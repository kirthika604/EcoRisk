EcoRisk AI — Learning How Human Activity Erodes Natural Disaster Resilience

Instead of studying one disaster, we trained a model on how human activity changes the land across multiple sites in the Himalayas and coastal India — it learned, on its own, that vegetation loss and construction growth are the strongest predictors of disaster severity, and correctly flags high-risk sites without being told what happened there.

1 · The Claim

What we set out to show, before any data is presented.
Human-driven land change is a measurable, model-confirmed amplifier of disaster risk, validated across multiple real sites spanning two different hazard types (slow-onset landslide/subsidence and acute cyclone), not asserted from a single anecdote.
Earlier version of this project made this case for one site (Joshimath) alone. This page is the multi-site test of that same claim.

    5 · Honest Limits

    Stated upfront, not waiting for a judge to catch these.
    • Small sample. N=10 real sites (7 hill, 3 coastal). A small, honestly-validated result is more credible than an inflated claim on more data than we actually have.
    • Correlation, not causation. The model finds association between human-activity signals and disaster occurrence; it does not prove any specific event was caused by human activity. Read every output as a risk-amplification signal.
    • Cross-terrain result is 2 of 3, not 3 of 3. The hill-trained model correctly flagged both real coastal disasters but false-positived on the one coastal control site.
    • Coastal disasters here are driven by acute cyclone intensity, not a slow land-use trend. Real NDBI/mangrove data was checked and does not cleanly separate coastal disaster from control sites: a mechanistically different hazard type from the hill-site story, not a weaker version of it.
    • Control-site selection is a judgment call. Each "no disaster" site was chosen and justified individually (see the site notes below), not randomly sampled.
